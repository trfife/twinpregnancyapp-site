With one baby, sleep advice tells you to "sleep when the baby sleeps." With twins, that only works if both babies sleep at the same time. That single idea—syncing your twins—is the difference between rest and round-the-clock exhaustion. Here's how to build a twin sleep schedule that actually gives you a break.

The golden rule: wake the sleeping twin

It feels wrong, but it's one of the most-used twin sleep strategies. When one baby wakes to feed, gently wake the other and feed them too. If you let them run on separate clocks, you'll never be off duty. Keeping their feeds and naps close together gives you a better chance of a break when they both sleep.

This applies day and night. Two babies on the same schedule is the whole game.

A sample newborn day (0–3 months)

Newborns feed often, about every 2 to 3 hours, and are awake only for short stretches. A loose, synced rhythm might look like this:

  • 7:00 am — Both babies wake and feed together
  • 7:45 am — Both down for a nap
  • 10:00 am — Feed both, short awake window, nap
  • Repeat the feed–awake–sleep cycle through the day
  • Evening — Shared bath or wind-down, then a "dream feed" for both before you sleep
  • Overnight — When one wakes to feed, feed the other too

Don't aim for clock-perfect. Aim for "together." A flexible synced schedule beats a rigid solo one every time.

Should twins share a crib?

US and UK guidance differ. In the US, the AAP recommends a separate sleep space for each multiple-birth baby and advises against co-bedding twins, in the hospital and at home. In the UK, the Lullaby Trust says newborn twins can share a cot, side by side or head to head on their backs, but need separate sleep spaces once they can roll. Both agree each baby sleeps on their back on a firm, flat surface with nothing loose in it. Follow the guidance where you live and ask your pediatrician.

Handling night wakings with two

  • Tag-team when you can. If you have a partner, take a baby each for the worst stretch, then trade nights.
  • Soloing? Feed in tandem. A twin nursing pillow lets you breastfeed both at once. With bottles, hold each one yourself: the NHS says never to leave a baby to feed alone with a propped-up bottle.
  • Keep the lights low. Dim, boring, and quiet teaches both babies that night is for sleeping.
  • Expect mismatched nights. One twin may sleep through while the other wakes. Syncing helps, but they're still two different people.

When sleep gets more predictable

As the months pass, awake times lengthen and naps settle into more of a pattern, at each baby's own pace. This is a natural time to firm up the schedule: a consistent bedtime, a short repeatable routine (feed, sleep sack, white noise, down), and the same nap anchors for both babies. Keep syncing—two babies on one rhythm stays the goal well into the first year.

Be kind to yourself

Twin sleep is hard, and "synced" doesn't mean "solved." Some nights both babies are up; some weeks a growth spurt rewrites everything. Protect your own rest where you can, accept help, and remember that getting twins onto the same clock is a skill you'll build over weeks, not days.
