Best car seats for twins

Buying car seats for twins is the one purchase you can't skip and can't delay—each baby needs their own rear-facing seat for the first ride home from the hospital, and the AAP suggests working with a certified child passenger safety technician before your babies are born. The good news: you don't need the most expensive seats on the shelf. You need two seats that fit your babies, fit each other side by side, and fit your actual car. Here's how to choose without second-guessing.

Start with two infant car seats

For the newborn months, most twin parents choose two infant (rear-facing, carrier-style) seats. They click out of a base so you can carry a sleeping baby inside, snap onto a compatible double stroller, and move one baby between cars without re-installing. With twins, that convenience is worth a lot—you'll be doing everything twice.

Check each seat's minimum weight and height on the label, since twins often arrive smaller and earlier than single babies. The AAP says very small babies usually fit better in rear-facing-only seats, and that babies born preterm should be screened in the hospital to make sure they can ride safely semi-reclined.

Two bases, two cars, or both?

If you have two drivers and two cars, consider an extra base for each seat so you're never re-installing in a parking lot with two crying babies. Bases are cheaper than whole seats and stay buckled in.

Will two seats fit across your back row?

This is the question that trips up twin parents. Two infant seats plus their bases can be a tight squeeze—especially in a sedan. Before you buy, measure the width of each seat at its widest point and add them together, then compare to your bench.

  • Two across, no third seat: Many infant seats fit side by side in the outboard positions. Test-fit if you can.
  • Two across plus an older child: If a sibling also needs a seat, you'll want narrow ("slim") seats and may need three across.
  • Check the buckles: Seats can technically fit but block each other's belt or LATCH anchors. Confirm you can actually buckle both.

When to switch to convertible seats

Rear-facing-only seats are outgrown when a baby reaches the seat's weight or height limit; the AAP lists 22 to 35 pounds and 26 to 35 inches, depending on the model. From there, twins move to convertible seats installed rear-facing, and the AAP says to keep them rear-facing as long as possible, until they reach the seat's highest rear-facing weight or height. For twins, the magic word is narrow. A slim convertible makes "three across" possible if you add a sibling, and keeps two seats from crowding each other.

Buy two identical convertibles

Matching seats install the same way and take the guesswork out of daily life. Once you've learned one, you've learned both—and you can swap accessories or covers between them.

Small extras that make twin car trips easier

  • Two car seat mirrors so you can glance at each rear-facing baby, but only ones your seat's manufacturer approves: the AAP says not to add products that didn't come with the seat or aren't approved by its maker.
  • Window shades to keep afternoon sun off two faces at once.
  • Seat protectors under each seat, again only if the car seat manufacturer approves them.

Get both seats checked

A check by a certified child passenger safety technician is worth booking; the AAP suggests doing it before your babies are born. Ask your hospital or pediatrician where to find one. Bring both seats and your vehicle—techs will confirm the angle, the tightness, and that two seats coexist safely in your specific car. Do this before your due date, because twins love to arrive early.

A quick buying checklist

  • Two rear-facing seats whose labels fit small, early babies
  • Measured to fit side by side in your actual back row
  • Extra bases if you have two cars or two drivers
  • A plan to move to two narrow convertibles, still rear-facing, when they outgrow the infant seats
  • Both seats checked by a certified technician before the birth

Choose seats that fit your babies and your car, install them early, and get them checked. With two seats buckled and ready, one of the biggest "bringing twins home" worries is already handled.
